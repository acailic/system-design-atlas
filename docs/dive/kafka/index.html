Kafka: the append-only log as an operating system for data

queue-logdistributedstorage-enginedifficulty: demandinglicense: Apache-2.0

Pinned commit: 3de09ca09784900318b56091d46d3803c4d5adc9 · 2026-10-10, trunk · upstream ↗

Kafka became the nervous system of every large data platform with a design inversion: instead of a smart message router, it is a dumb, append-only, partitioned commit log that exploits the one thing disks and networks do at full speed — sequential transfer — and pushes intelligence (what to consume, how fast, where you are) into clients. This deep-dive teaches the throughput architecture (batching, zero-copy, sparse indexes), the replication machinery (ISR + high watermark), and the newest lesson: Kafka's own metadata became an event-sourced raft log (KRaft), the system eating its own dog food.

🧒 Explain like I'm 10

A conveyor belt of numbered boxes. Kafka is a very long belt that only moves forward (the append-only log). Senders drop sealed boxes onto it (batches of records), and each box gets a number as it lands (its offset). Every reader (a consumer group) walks the belt at their own pace — fast walkers and slow walkers never collide — and each remembers their box number in a little notebook (committed offsets... stored on ANOTHER belt, because everything in Kafka is a belt). The belt is long (retention), so a brand-new reader can go back to box 1 and replay everything. Copies of the belt run at friends' houses (replicas), and a special marker (the high watermark) shows how far the belt has been copied — readers may only read up to the marker, never past it.

Why the grown-ups did it this way: belts (disks and networks) are fastest when moving in one direction without stopping — and storing each reader's position as data means nobody has to keep a giant "who received what" list.

1. Architecture overview

  • The spine — one produce — is: handleProduceRequest (core/src/main/scala/kafka/server/KafkaApis.scala:401) → ReplicaManager.appendRecords (638) → partition leader → UnifiedLog.appendAsLeader (storage/src/main/java/org/apache/kafka/storage/internals/log/UnifiedLog.java:1062) → LogSegment.append (LogSegment.java:250) writes batches sequentially and appends sparse index entries; with acks=all the response waits in purgatory until followers replicate.
  • A partition is the unit of everything: ordering, leadership, retention, parallelism. Topics are just names over partition sets.

Walkthrough: one produce with acks=all, then a consumer reads it

The spine as a story — step through it on the site or read straight down. Watch where the record becomes durable and where it becomes visible: those are two different moments.

1

The batch leaves

The producer groups records into a compressed batch with a base offset and CRC — the batch is the unit of send, of append, and of retry, so throughput is paid once, everywhere.

2

Into the broker

An acceptor hands the connection to a processor thread (NIO selector, network/SocketServer.scala:71); the request lands on a queue consumed by a KafkaRequestHandler (server/KafkaRequestHandler.scala:91), which calls KafkaApis.handleProduceRequest (401).

3

Append to the leader

ReplicaManager.appendRecords (638) routes to the partition leader; UnifiedLog.appendAsLeader (1062) takes the per-partition lock — one partition's append is serial, and that serialness IS the ordering guarantee — and LogSegment.append (250) writes the batch sequentially, adding one sparse index entry per ~4KB.

4

Wait in purgatory

With acks=all, the produce response parks in DelayedOperationPurgatory (server-common/.../purgatory/, 38) — a timer wheel plus per-key watchers — until the ISR replicates the record or the request times out.

5

Followers copy the belt

ReplicaFetcherThreads (30) fetch from the leader exactly like consumers; the leader truncates divergent tails using leader epochs (zombie-leader fencing). When the in-sync set has the record, maybeIncrementLeaderHW (cluster/Partition.scala:1012) moves the high watermark.

6

Ack, and only then visible

Purgatory completes the parked response → the producer's ack returns. Consumers fetching see data only up to the high watermark (handleFetchRequest 568 → LogSegment.read 435), served from the page cache to the socket by zero-copy transferFrom (FileRecords.writeTo:280).

✓

Durable, then visible

One batch is now: (a) on the leader's disk, (b) on every in-sync replica, (c) readable by any consumer — and replayable forever by any NEW consumer that starts at offset 0. The dumb belt did all the work.

2. Core data structures — and why these

The log segment trio: .log, .index, .timeIndex

(storage/.../log/LogSegment.java:65)

Data lives in immutable-ish segment files (rolled by size/time); appends go only to the active one (LogSegment.append:250). The offset index is sparse — every ~4KB of data, one (relativeOffset, position) pair — mmapped (AbstractIndex.java:72) and searched by binary search (AbstractIndex.java:501-513). Why sparse: an entry per byte would itself be big data; binary search over a sparse index + one sequential scan of ≤4KB bounds any offset lookup. This is the entire "database" of the consumer side.

Record batches (clients/.../record/)

Producers group records into compressed batches with base offsets and CRCs; the broker treats a batch as the unit of append, replication, and validation. Why batches: throughput everywhere — fewer syscalls, fewer network frames, compression amortization. The batch is also the transaction and idempotence unit (producer epochs, sequence numbers).

The high watermark and leader epochs

(core/.../cluster/Partition.scala:1012/1091)

Each partition's leader advances the high watermark (HW) — the offset replicated to all in-sync replicas (maybeIncrementLeaderHW:1012) — and shrinks the ISR on follower timeouts (maybeShrinkIsr:1091). Consumers see only ≤HW data. Why: one number reconciles "durable enough" with "readable now" across replicas without distributed transactions.

Delayed-operation purgatory

(server-common/.../purgatory/DelayedOperationPurgatory.java:38)

acks=all produces and timed fetches park in purgatory — a timer wheel

  • key-indexed watchers structure — until replication satisfies them or they time out. Why: waiting is the common case in a replicated log; making it O(1)-ish per event (not per poll) is what keeps tail latency sane.

3. Concurrency and failure model

  • Threading is role-partitioned per broker: acceptor → processor threads (NIO selectors, one per listener group, network/SocketServer.scala:71) own sockets; a request queue feeds a pool of KafkaRequestHandlers (server/KafkaRequestHandler.scala:91, run loop at 108); each partition's log has its own lock — so appends to different partitions on one broker proceed in parallel, and one partition's append is serial (the ordering guarantee is the lock).
  • Replication: followers run ReplicaFetcherThreads (server/ReplicaFetcherThread.scala:30) fetching from the leader's log like consumers; the leader truncates followers' divergent tails using leader epochs (fencing zombie leaders). Failure → the controller (now QuorumController on the KRaft raft log, metadata/.../QuorumController.java:177) elects an ISR member.
  • Durability is config: acks + min.insync.replicas define the replication barrier; unclean leader election is (by default) refused — consistency over availability, the deliberate opposite of AP systems.
  • Consumers hold their position: offsets are committed into the __consumer_offsets topic (log all the way down); a group rebalances partition ownership on membership changes — the new-generation coordinator is itself an event-sourced state machine in group-coordinator/.../GroupCoordinatorShard.java.

4. API and extension design

  • The wire API is versioned request/response schemas (generated from JSON): brokers negotiate versions with clients — a rolling- upgrade contract that has kept a decade of mixed client/broker versions interoperating.
  • The consumer/producer are client libraries, not protocol minions: batching, compression, partitioners, and retry policy live client-side (clients/ module — the same Java code brokers use).
  • Extension points are storage-level: tiered storage (storage/.../remote/), pluggable serializers/connect API (separate repo), and the log-compact ("compacted") retention mode that turns a topic into a key→latest-value table.
  • Honest wart: rebalancing consumer groups has been the operational sore spot for a decade — the new KIP-848 protocol exists because global coordination on the hot path is genuinely hard.

5. The defining trade-offs

Trade-off 1: dumb sequential log + zero-copy over a "smart" store

  • Tension: millions of messages/sec on commodity disks and 10GbE.
  • Choice: append-only segments; sparse index maps offsets to positions; serving a fetch copies bytes from page cache to socket with transferFrom/sendfile (clients/.../record/internal/FileRecords.java:writeTo:280) — zero-copy, no user-space buffer churn.
  • Evidence: LogSegment.append:250 (sequential), AbstractIndex mmap+binarySearch (501-513), FileRecords.writeTo:280 (destChannel.transferFrom).
  • Given up: key-based random access (it's an offset log, not a database — consumers wanting keys build tables or use compaction), and disk-space discipline via retention policies instead of deletes.
  • Verdict: right when the workload is a firehose with replay; wrong for point lookups — which is why Kafka Streams builds state stores on top of the log, not inside it.

Trade-off 2: pull-based consumers with committed positions over push

  • Tension: deliver events without overrunning slow consumers.
  • Choice: consumers fetch at their own pace; positions are data (committed to a topic); groups split partitions among members.
  • Evidence: fetch path handleFetchRequest:568 → LogSegment.read:435 slices from the HW; offsets committed to __consumer_offsets; GroupCoordinatorShard (event-sourced coordinator).
  • Given up: push latency floor (poll interval), and a nontrivial client (every consumer embeds a mini distributed system for rebalancing).
  • Verdict: backpressure for free and replayability forever — the two properties that made Kafka an integration backbone. Push systems (NATS core, MQTT) win on latency-to-slow-clients, and lose replay.

Trade-off 3: ISR + high watermark replication over quorum commits

  • Tension: replicate durably without slowing every write to the slowest replica.
  • Choice: write to the leader + all in-sync replicas (acks=all); ISR membership is elastic (maybeShrinkIsr:1091); the high watermark gates consumer visibility (maybeIncrementLeaderHW:1012).
  • Evidence: both functions in core/.../cluster/Partition.scala; leader-epoch fencing in ReplicaFetcherThread.scala:30.
  • Given up: strict majority quorums — a mis-set min.insync.replicas or unclean election can lose data (the default forbids it, choosing unavailability).
  • Verdict: tuned for throughput with tunable durability; the same dial Raft fixes with consensus — which Kafka itself adopted for its metadata (KRaft), where correctness beats throughput.

6. Reading path

  1. core/.../server/KafkaApis.scala:handleProduceRequest (401) — the front door: validation, idempotence/txn checks, the local-vs-remote partition dispatch.
  2. core/.../server/ReplicaManager.scala:appendRecords (638) and appendRecordsToLeader (586) — where replication policy meets the log; note the purgatory handoff for acks=all.
  3. storage/.../log/UnifiedLog.java:appendAsLeader (1062) — per- partition lock, segment roll policy, index upkeep; then LogSegment.append (250): the actual sequential write + index entry append.
  4. storage/.../log/AbstractIndex.java — mmap (72), sparse-entry layout, binary search (501-513); run kafka-dump-log on a test segment if you want to see it live.
  5. Fetch side: KafkaApis.handleFetchRequest (568) → LogSegment.read (435) → FileRecords slicing; then FileRecords.writeTo (280) — the zero-copy send to the socket.
  6. server-common/.../purgatory/DelayedOperationPurgatory.java (38) — how waiting requests complete on replication events instead of polling.
  7. core/.../cluster/Partition.scala — maybeIncrementLeaderHW (1012) and maybeShrinkIsr (1091): trace how one produce becomes visible to consumers.
  8. core/.../server/ReplicaFetcherThread.scala (30, processPartitionData at 113) — followers are consumers; epoch fencing on truncation.
  9. group-coordinator/.../GroupCoordinatorShard.java — the new coordinator as event-sourced state machine (metadata events in, responses out).
  10. core/.../server/KafkaRaftServer.scala (49) + metadata/.../QuorumController.java (177) — brokers and the controller both boot from the same raft metadata log; the controller is a deterministic function over metadata events.
  11. core/.../network/SocketServer.scala (71) + core/.../server/KafkaRequestHandler.scala (91) — the broker's thread architecture: selectors vs handler pool, one queue in between.

You have now traced one message from producer batch to replicated, zero-copied, consumer-visible record — and seen how Kafka rebuilt its own control plane on the same log abstraction.

7. What to steal

  • Make the sequential log your storage primitive: batching + zero-copy + sparse indexes is a repeatable throughput recipe, not a Kafka accident.
  • Offsets as data: storing consumer positions in the log itself removed a whole class of coordinator state (compare: external ZK offsets, the thing it replaced).
  • Backpressure by pull: consumers that fetch control their own fate; no broker-side queue accounting per slow client.
  • Version every wire message: generated, negotiated schemas made a decade of rolling upgrades survivable — most systems die here.
  • Event-source your control plane (KRaft/QuorumController): deterministic functions over an append-only metadata log replace fragile external consensus — the deepest pattern in this codebase.

8. Difficulty and prerequisites

  • Difficulty: demanding — three languages of concern (Scala plumbing, Java log engine, generated protocol), plus distributed systems vocabulary.
  • Budget: ~8 hours for the reading path; replication semantics need a second pass.
  • Prerequisites: what a commit log is; replication and quorums at a textbook level; Java/Scala reading fluency; mmap basics.
